\section{Part I --- The Deutsche Bank Case Study}

\subsection*{Setup and conventions}
Settlement date: \textbf{August 15, 2003}. Exhibit 1 lists 52 Treasury coupon
bonds whose maturities fall on successive semi-annual dates (Feb 15 / Aug 15)
from 2004 to 2029 with no gaps, i.e.\ exactly one bond at each of the nodes
$t=0.5,1.0,\dots,26.0$ years. This allows the classic \emph{successive
substitution} bootstrapping method.

\textbf{Compounding convention.} Exhibit 4's footnote states explicitly that
Deutsche Bank's own model yields are \emph{Bond-Equivalent Yields} (BEY),
i.e.\ twice the semi-annual compounded yield. Since U.S.\ Treasuries pay
semi-annual coupons, we derive our own zero-coupon yields in the identical
BEY convention throughout, so that every comparison against the DB model in
Q1.2 is apples-to-apples.
$$\text{BEY} = 2\left(D^{-1/k}-1\right), \qquad D = \text{discount factor to period } k \text{ (6-month steps)}.$$

%--------------------------------------------------------------------
\question{Q1.1 --- Bootstrapping the discount curve}

\textbf{Methodology.} For bond $k$ (maturing at period $k$, coupon $c$ per
annum, price $P_k$):
$$P_k = \frac{c}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} D_j + \left(\frac{c}{2}+100\right)D_k
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
D_k = \frac{P_k - \frac{c}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}D_j}{\frac{c}{2}+100}.$$
Solved in order of increasing maturity, using only already-known $D_j$, $j<k$.

\textbf{Validation.} Our bootstrapped curve matches Deutsche Bank's own
Exhibit 4 model yields \emph{exactly} at 2Y (1.873\%) and 10Y (4.711\%) 
i.e.\ the maturities the case identifies as DB's own calibration points
for its three-factor model. This is strong internal validation they were 
appropriate methods to use.

\question{Q1.2 --- Comparison with Deutsche Bank's model, trading signals}

\textbf{Logic.} Yield and price move in opposite directions. If our
bootstrapped (market-implied) yield is \emph{higher} than DB's model yield,
the bond's market price is below the model's ``fair'' price $\Rightarrow$ the
bond is \textbf{cheap} $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{BUY}. If the market yield is
\emph{lower}, the bond is \textbf{expensive} $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{SELL}.
We keep a $\pm 1 bps$ margin where the signal remains as "fair".

%--------------------------------------------------------------------
\question{Q1.3 --- Is this risk-free arbitrage?}

Although the strategy is directional and model-based, it is \textbf{not}
a riskless arbitrage, for several reasons:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Model risk.} The ``mispricing'' is only a mispricing relative
to Deutsche Bank's three-factor model. If the model is
mis-specified (wrong number of factors, wrong functional form, stale
calibration), the signal could simply be wrong. 
\item \textbf{Convergence/timing risk.} Even if the model is correctly
specified on average, there is no guarantee \emph{when}, or even
\emph{whether}, market yields converge back toward the model yields within
the trader's holding horizon. The position carries mark-to-market
risk.
\item \textbf{No riskless, capital-structure-free payoff.} A true arbitrage
requires a replicating, self-financing portfolio with a knowable, riskless
terminal payoff. Here the ``trade'' is a directional bet on convergence, 
funded at some financing rate, with basis risk (signal on a Zero Coupon 
vs. trading on Coupon Bonds) between the position and any hedge.
\item \textbf{Curve risk while waiting.} Between trade inception and
convergence, the \emph{whole} curve can move (parallel shifts, twists), and
unless the position is duration/key-rate hedged, those moves can override the small
model-implied edge of a few basis points.

\end{itemize}
In short, this is a \textbf{relative-value} trade: a model-informed bet with
positive expected value under the trader's own beliefs about the model's
validity, not a riskless arbitrage in the classical sense.

\question{Q2.1 --- Cubic spline of the zero-coupon curve}

\textbf{Methodology.} Natural cubic spline (zero second derivative at the
boundary knots) through the five knots specified (2, 5, 10, 15, 20 years),
fit directly on the Q1.1 bootstrapped BEY yields. Natural endpoints are an
explicit modelling choice, not SciPy's default. Outside $[2,20]$y the first
or last cubic polynomial is continued; the tails are not generally linear.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{q2_spline_vs_bootstrap.png}
\caption{Cubic spline (red) vs. raw bootstrapped zero yields (grey dots). Shaded regions are extrapolated.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Fit quality.} For the 37 observations within $[2,20]$y, including
the five anchors, the residual (spline minus bootstrap) has mean
$-5.50$\,bps, sample standard deviation $8.28$\,bps and maximum absolute
value $29.91$\,bps, attained at 11.5Y with a negative sign.
The spline interpolates five selected yields exactly; it does not
statistically estimate or average away noise. The high-coupon bonds cited
in Exhibit 1 mature at 10.5--12Y, so they cannot determine earlier 6--9Y
bootstrap yields in a successive-substitution procedure. The supplied
evidence does not establish issuance vintage, liquidity or callability as
the cause of the residuals.

\question{Q2.2 --- Does using the spline change the Q1.2 conclusions?}

Spline estimates are useful at maturities without an observed point and
when a continuous curve is required for valuation or scenario analysis.
Replacing available market-implied yields with a five-anchor approximation
is a modelling choice; the spline does not exactly reprice all input bonds.

We repeat the Q1.2 comparison using the \textbf{spline-implied} yield
instead of the bootstrap. The spline must match the five anchors
(2,5,10,15,20Y) and may differ elsewhere. The table retains BUY/SELL as
\emph{conditional, model-based labels}, with the Q1.2 reporting band:
these are comparisons of curve estimates against DB, not new market quotes.

\textbf{This matters a great deal.} Six of the thirteen signals (1, 6, 7, 8,
9, 25 years) \emph{flip sign} once the spline replaces the raw bootstrap.
Two distinct mechanisms are at work:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Extrapolation risk (1Y and 25Y).} These points are outside
$[2,20]$y. Their spline estimates depend on end-cubic continuation, so the
resulting comparisons require explicit tail assumptions; extrapolation
alone does not prove that an estimate is invalid.
\item \textbf{Interpolation effect (6--9Y).} At 6, 7, 8 and 9Y, the spline
lies respectively $3.78$, $10.08$, $15.03$ and $14.05$ bp below the bootstrap.
These differences exceed the corresponding positive bootstrap-minus-DB
gaps and reverse their signs. This is an effect of the five-anchor
interpolation, not evidence that later high-coupon bonds caused noise in
the earlier bootstrap yields.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Conclusion.} The comparison is sensitive to curve construction.
Substituting spline estimates for market-implied yields changes the object
being compared with DB. The six reversals do not establish that the
bootstrap is erroneous, that the spline removes noise or that a spline-based
SELL is superior to the original BUY candidate. Trading conclusions require
consistent cash-flow pricing and an assessment of model risk and costs.

\question{Q3.1 --- Nelson-Siegel-Svensson fit}
 
\textbf{What is estimated.} The question asks for the \emph{discount function}. We estimate it in
spot-rate space and map back, keeping the Q1.1 bond-equivalent-yield convention throughout so that
every comparison with the bootstrap is apples-to-apples:
\[
  D(m)=\Bigl(1+\tfrac{y(m)}{2}\Bigr)^{-2m},
  \qquad
  y(m)=\beta_0+\beta_1\Lambda_1(m)+\beta_2\Lambda_2(m)+\beta_3\Lambda_3(m),
\]
\[
  \Lambda_1(m)=\frac{1-e^{-m/\tau_1}}{m/\tau_1},
  \qquad
  \Lambda_2(m)=\Lambda_1(m)-e^{-m/\tau_1},
  \qquad
  \Lambda_3(m)=\frac{1-e^{-m/\tau_2}}{m/\tau_2}-e^{-m/\tau_2}.
\]
Setting $\beta_3=0$ gives the three-factor Nelson-Siegel (NS) model; retaining it gives the
four-factor Svensson (NSS) extension. $\Lambda_1$ decays monotonically from $1$ to $0$, so $\beta_1$
is a short-end (slope) factor; $\Lambda_2$ is zero at both ends and peaks at $m^\star=1.7933\,\tau_1$,
so $\beta_2$ bends only the belly; the loading on $\beta_0$ is constant, so $\beta_0=y(\infty)$ is the
asymptotic long rate.
 
\textbf{Methodology.} Estimating $(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3,\tau_1,\tau_2)$ jointly by
unconstrained nonlinear least squares proved numerically fragile: different starting points converged
to different, equally-well-fitting local optima. The cause is a near-linear dependence among the
loadings once $\tau_1$ is large relative to the sample. Regressing the curvature loading on the other
two over our $0.5$--$26$y grid:
 
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
$\tau_1$ & $R^2$ of $\Lambda_2$ on $[1,\Lambda_1]$ & $\operatorname{cond}(X)$ & $\operatorname{corr}(\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2)$ \\
\midrule
 2.00 y & 0.389 & 19.0 & $+0.62$ \\
  5.00 y & 0.099 & 22.5 & $-0.31$ \\
12.08 y & \textbf{0.905} & \textbf{60.8} & $\mathbf{-0.95}$ \\
20.00 y & 0.976 & 139.5 & $-0.99$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
 
\noindent At $\tau_1\approx12$y the curvature loading is over $90\%$ explained by a linear combination
of the level and slope loadings, so the three betas are not separately identified.
 
We therefore \textbf{concentrate the betas out of the objective} (profiled, or variable-projection,
least squares). Conditional on $(\tau_1,\tau_2)$ the loadings are fixed basis functions of maturity,
the betas enter linearly, and the inner problem is an OLS regression with a unique closed-form
solution; only the low-dimensional $\tau$ problem is searched, on a grid of
$\tau_1\in[0.3,25]$y (500 points, step $0.049$y) for the three-factor model
and a $80\times80$ grid over $\tau_1\in[0.3,15]$y, $\tau_2\in[2,30]$y for Svensson, excluding
$|\tau_1-\tau_2|<0.5$y to avoid the degenerate $\tau_1\simeq\tau_2$ corner. This is the estimator of Nelson \&
Siegel (1987), systematised by Gilli, Gro\ss e \& Schumann (2010). Diebold \& Li (2006) instead
\emph{fix} $\lambda=1/\tau_1=0.0609$ a priori --- placing the curvature peak at 30 months --- so that
no search is needed at all; we borrow that device below.
 
\textbf{Estimation choices (documented).}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Objective.} Equally weighted squared errors on \emph{zero-coupon yields}, not on
        prices. Yield-space weighting treats every maturity alike; price-space weighting would let
        the long end dominate through its duration and would effectively re-weight towards the
        legacy high-coupon bonds identified in Q2.1.
  \item \textbf{Convention.} BEY (semi-annual) throughout, matching Q1.1--Q1.2.
  \item \textbf{Sample.} All 52 bootstrapped nodes, $m=0.5,\dots,26$y. No outlier is removed: the
        9--13y pricing anomaly is left in so that its effect on the fit is visible.
  \item \textbf{$\tau$ selection.} Profiled RSS minimisation, \emph{plus} a constrained variant with
        $\tau_1$ restricted to the conventional short-end range. Both are reported, for the reason
        given under ``Identification'' below.
  \item \textbf{Factor count.} Three versus four, decided below.
\end{enumerate}
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{q3_nss_fit.png}
  \caption{Nelson-Siegel fit vs.\ the Q1.1 bootstrapped curve.}
  \label{fig:ns-fit}
\end{figure}
 
\textbf{Identification: the profiled optimum is not economically interpretable.} The profiled RSS
surface is nearly flat in $\tau_1$, while the betas move enormously along that ridge:
 
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
$\tau_1$ & $\beta_0$ (level) & $\beta_1$ (slope) & $\beta_2$ (curvature) & RMSE \\
\midrule

\noindent The profiled optimum does fit better --- by $4.1$ bps of RMSE against the best fit with an
interpretable $\tau_1$ --- but it buys that fit by abandoning the factor interpretation entirely.
Taken at face value it states that the asymptotic long rate is $0.695\%$, against a bootstrapped

Against that, $4.1$ bps has to be judged on the scale of the errors this fit already carries: the
maximum residual is $32.5$ bps at $10.5$Y, in the 9--13y window that Q2.1 attributes to
idiosyncratic legacy-bond pricing rather than to curve shape. The extra fit bought by a free

that means nothing --- is not. We report both fits and carry out the Q3.2 decomposition on the
identified one.
 
\textbf{Model selection: three or four factors?} The plain three-factor NS model achieves
RMSE $=7.96$ bps; the four-factor Svensson improves this to $7.28$ bps
--- but it does so with
\[
  \beta_0=-13.938\%,\quad \beta_1=+14.302\%,\quad \beta_2=+8.837\%,\quad
  \beta_3=+54.892\%,\quad \tau_1=4.022\text{y},\quad \tau_2=16.886\text{y}.
\]
Formal tests favour the larger model: a nested $F$-test rejects NS at the $5\%$ level
($F(2,46)=4.52$, $p=0.016$), and both AIC ($-734.1$ vs.\ $-739.4$) and BIC ($-726.3$ vs.\ $-727.7$)
prefer Svensson.
 
We adopt the three-factor model regardless, because the Svensson parameter vector is not a
description of a second hump. Its asymptotic long rate is \emph{negative} ($-13.94\%$), held up by
an offsetting $\beta_1$ of $+14.30\%$; and its second curvature factor carries a loading of
$54.89\%$ with $\tau_2=16.886$y, placing that hump's peak at $1.7933\,\tau_2=30.3$ years --- four
years beyond the longest bond in the sample. The fourth factor is thus a second \emph{out-of-sample}
level factor, not a second hump: it is the same identification pathology as above, now with two
decay parameters instead of one. The $0.68$ bps it buys also sits well inside the pricing noise
documented in Q2.1, where residuals around the early-1980s high-coupon bonds reach $30$ bps. The
$F$-test is measuring in-sample flexibility, not economic content.

\question{Q3.2 --- Factor loadings and economic drivers}
 
\textbf{The loadings.} The three loadings are, by construction, orthogonal in \emph{shape} rather
than in value: $\Lambda_0\equiv1$ is flat and shifts every maturity equally; $\Lambda_1$ falls
monotonically from $1$ at $m=0$ to $0$ at $m=\infty$ and therefore moves only the short end; and
$\Lambda_2$ is a hump, zero at both ends, peaking at
\[
  m^\star = 1.7933\,\tau_1 ,
\]
so it tilts the belly without disturbing either asymptote. This is what licenses the
level/slope/curvature reading, and it holds only when $m^\star$ sits comfortably \emph{inside} the
sample.
 
\textbf{Why the profiled decomposition cannot be interpreted.} With $\tau_1=12.08$y the curvature
loading peaks at $m^\star=21.7$ years and has fallen back only to $0.294$ by $m=26$ (it reaches
$0.12$ at 100y and $0.02$ at 500y). Over the \emph{observed} range it is therefore monotone
increasing and near-flat at the long end --- visually and statistically a second level/slope factor
rather than a hump. The regression reported in Q3.1 quantifies this: $\Lambda_2$ is $90.5\%$
explained by $[1,\Lambda_1]$. The least-squares step consequently loads almost all the explanatory
power onto $\beta_2$ ($17.9\%$), leaving a level of $0.695\%$ and a slope of $-0.140\%$ that bear no
relation to the curve's actual asymptote ($5.9\%$) or actual slope ($+500$ bps). The profiled fit is
an excellent \emph{interpolant} and a meaningless \emph{decomposition}; Figure~\ref{fig:loadings-profiled}
shows exactly this. We therefore decompose the identified fit of Table~\ref{tab:ns-params}.

\textbf{Economic drivers.} Settlement is 15 August 2003, and each factor maps onto a distinct
macro-financial driver observable on that date.
 
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Level ($\beta_0=6.913\%$) --- long-run nominal anchor.} The asymptotic rate is the
        sum of expected long-run real growth, expected long-run inflation and a term premium. None
        of these responds to the business cycle, which is why the loading is flat: a repricing of
        trend inflation moves the entire curve. Note that $6.91\%$ sits about $100$ bps above the
        longest yield we observe ($5.905\%$ at 26Y): with $\tau_1$ short, the model extrapolates
        the still-rising mid-curve out to infinity. $\beta_0$ should therefore be read as the
        model's long-run anchor, not as a rate quoted anywhere in the market.
  \item \textbf{Slope ($\beta_1=-6.260\%$) --- monetary policy stance.} $\beta_1=y(0)-y(\infty)$,
        so a large negative $\beta_1$ is a very steep curve. In August 2003 the Fed funds target
        stood at $1.00\%$ after the June 2003 cut, the trough of the post-dot-com easing cycle and
        the lowest policy rate in 45 years, set against an explicit deflation scare earlier that
        year. The fit implies $y(0)=\beta_0+\beta_1=0.654\%$, within $23$ bps of our bootstrapped
        6-month yield of $0.882\%$ and consistent with a $1\%$ policy rate. The slope factor is
        therefore measuring the gap between an emergency-low policy rate and an unchanged long-run
        anchor: $626$ bps of expected normalisation.
  \item \textbf{Curvature ($\beta_2=-4.598\%$, peaking at $3.3$y) --- the expected path, not the
        endpoints.} Curvature captures the \emph{timing} of the normalisation priced by the slope
        factor, and its sign here is informative: $\beta_2<0$ pushes the 2--5y sector \emph{below}
        the path implied by level and slope alone, i.e.\ the market expects the Fed to stay low
        first and move later, rather than to normalise at a constant exponential rate. That is
        precisely the guidance in force at settlement: on 12 August 2003, three days before our
        valuation date, the FOMC held the funds target at $1\%$ and stated that policy
        accommodation could be maintained ``for a considerable period'' --- language that Fed funds
        futures read as a commitment not to tighten for at least six months. A delayed-liftoff path
        depresses the front belly exactly where $\Lambda_2$ loads most heavily.
 
        The same sector is where convexity flows were concentrated: between mid-June and mid-August
        2003 the 10Y yield rose roughly 150 bps as mortgage servicers sold duration into a falling
        market. That is the 5--10y region in which our residuals are largest ($+32.5$ bps at
        $10.5$Y), which is why we read the curvature factor as the policy-path signal and treat the
        9--13y residual as bond-specific noise rather than curve shape.
\end{itemize}
 
\textbf{Relation to Deutsche Bank's model.} This is the same decomposition logic as DB's
three-factor model in Exhibit 3, and it explains why DB calibrates to the 2Y and 10Y points
(Q1.1): two maturities pin down slope and curvature given a level anchored by the long end. It also
explains the Q1.2 pattern. A three-factor model cannot reproduce idiosyncratic, bond-specific
deviations by construction, so any residual between DB's model and the market is either genuine
relative value or bond-specific noise --- and Q2.2 plus the 10Y discount-factor residual above both
point to the latter in the 6--9Y sector.
 
\textbf{Caveat carried forward.} The identification problem of Q3.1 is not an artefact of our
estimator; it is a property of fitting a three-factor exponential model on a sample whose maturity
range is not much longer than $\tau_1$. When $m^\star$ approaches the long end of the data, the
``curvature'' factor and a slow-moving long-rate factor become observationally equivalent. Any
practitioner tracking NS betas through time must therefore fix $\tau_1$ (as Diebold \& Li do) rather
than re-optimise it each period, or the resulting beta series will be contaminated by movements in
$\tau$ masquerading as movements in level and curvature.

\question{Q4.1 --- Shocking the 10Y key rate}

\textbf{Methodology.} The Q2.1 curve is a natural cubic spline through the
bootstrapped zero yields at the knots 2, 5, 10, 15 and 20y, which are exactly
the assignment's key rates. We therefore implement ``shock the 10-year rate by
50bps'' literally on that model: we add $+50$bps to the 10Y knot value,
\emph{re-fit} the natural spline, and define the curve shock as
$$\Delta y(t) = S_{\text{bumped}}(t) - S_{\text{base}}(t), \qquad t = 0.5, 1.0, \dots, 26\text{y}.$$
Because spline interpolation is linear in the knot values, $\Delta y(t)$ does
not depend on the level of the curve, and the five key-rate shocks sum
\emph{exactly} to a $+50$bps parallel shift at every maturity (verified in the
code). For comparison, we also run the textbook Ho (1992) triangular
(``tent'') shock: full size at its own knot, decaying linearly to zero at the
adjacent knots, and held flat beyond the outer knots.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{q4_1_shock_10y.png}
\caption{Left: the curve shock $\Delta y(t)$ from re-fitting the spline (red)
vs.\ the triangular tent (dashed); hollow markers are the four knots left
unchanged. Right: the five spline key-rate shocks. Shaded areas: extrapolated
maturities ($<2$y, $>20$y).}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Impact on the curve.} The shock is $+50$bps at 10Y and, by
construction, zero at the other knots (2, 5, 15, 20y). However, unlike the
tent, the spline response is \textbf{not local}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Between 5Y and 15Y it is \emph{wider} than the tent: $+22.1$bps at 7y
and $+37.0$bps at 12y, versus $+20$ and $+30$bps for the linear ramp.
\item Outside $[5,15]$y the cubic \emph{ripples} with alternating signs:
about $-3$ to $-4$bps between 3 and 4y, $-8.4$bps at 17y, and $+8.4$bps at 23y.
\item In the \textbf{extrapolated} region beyond 20y the spline continues the
last cubic polynomial --- the \texttt{CubicSpline} implementation extends the
end cubic, it does not hold the curve flat --- so the ripple grows to
$-11.5$bps at 26y. (A flat extension would put the 26Y yield at $5.95\%$
instead of $6.35\%$ and would set this last contribution to zero; we keep the
model-consistent extension and quantify the sensitivity in Q4.3.) The right
panel shows that this effect is much stronger for the outer knots: a 20Y bump
moves the 26y yield by about $+103$bps, a 15Y bump moves it by about
$-44$bps, and a 2Y bump moves the 0.5y yield by $+79$bps.
\end{itemize}

In short, a key-rate shock is only as local as the curve model it is applied
to. The tent is local by design, while a cubic spline spreads a single-node
shock across the whole curve, most strongly where the curve is extrapolated
rather than interpolated.

\question{Q4.2 --- Portfolio KRD to the 10Y shock}

\textbf{Portfolio construction (assumption).} ``Equally invested'' is read as
an equal \emph{dollar market value}: \$1,000,000/52 $\approx$ \$19{,}230.77
in each bond, i.e.\ a face amount of
$(\text{dollar amount}/\text{price})\times 100$ per bond. The alternative
reading, an equal \emph{face} amount (\$19{,}230.77 of face in each bond),
would give a \$1.19m market value and $\text{KRD}_{10Y}=2.31$y: equal face
overweights the cheap short bonds and underweights the expensive long ones,
so it raises duration. We use equal market value, the standard reading of
``equally invested'', and report the alternative in the robustness table.

\textbf{Approach.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item Build the $52\times52$ cash-flow matrix (semi-annual coupons plus
principal) and aggregate it into the portfolio cash flow at each semi-annual
date.
\item \emph{Base valuation} uses the Q1.1 bootstrapped zero curve (BEY,
semi-annual compounding). It reprices every bond exactly, so the base NAV is
exactly \$1,000,000. Pricing off the spline itself would give
\$1,002,389, and the \$2,389 gap (the Q2.2 fitting residual) would
contaminate the risk measure.
\item \emph{Shocked valuation}: add the spline-implied $\Delta y(t)$ from Q4.1
to the bootstrapped yields and reprice. The shape of the shock therefore
comes from the Q2.1 model, while the base level matches market prices.
\item Report the result as (i) the dollar P\&L of the $+50$bps shock;
(ii) the KRD in years, using a central difference to strip out convexity,
$\text{KRD}_k = -\dfrac{P(+h)-P(-h)}{2\,P_0\,h}$ with $h=50$bps; and
(iii) the DV01 (\$ per 1bp), which is the quantity used to size hedges.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Result.}
$$\boxed{\Delta P_{10Y} = \$989{,}186.86 - \$1{,}000{,}000.00 = -\$10{,}813.14
\;(-1.08\%\text{ of NAV}), \qquad \text{KRD}_{10Y} = 2.21\text{ years},
\qquad \text{DV01}_{10Y} = \$221.0}$$
The sign is as expected: a long bond portfolio loses value when yields rise.
The \$221.0 is a \emph{central}-difference DV01,
$\text{DV01}=-(P(+h)-P(-h))/2\times10^{-4}$; the \emph{one-sided} $+50$bps
P\&L implies \$216.26 per bp, so $\Delta P\neq-50\times\text{DV01}$. The
\$4.8 per bp gap is the convexity term, which a 50bp move is large enough to
reveal; we report the central figure because it is the one used to size the
hedge in Q4.3.

\textbf{What actually moves the number (robustness).} The KRD is essentially
invariant to the pricing engine and materially sensitive to the \emph{shock
design}, which is where most of the dispersion between reasonable
implementations comes from:

Every defensible convention puts the 10Y exposure between $2.0$ and $2.4$

weight on 20Y and far less on 15Y than a hedge sized on tent KRDs, even
though both imply the same total duration.

key-rate residuals are at most \$606 (20Y), from second-order convexity.

\subsection*{Data, event set and conventions}

\textbf{Data.} Daily US Treasury constant-maturity yields for ten maturities,
from 3 months to 30 years (FRED series \texttt{DGS3MO} to \texttt{DGS30}),
downloaded with the course helper \texttt{fred.py}. The sample used in Q1 runs from 2 January 2015 to 1 August 2025, i.e.\
2{,}647 trading days. Yields are in percent; all results are reported in basis
points (bp).

\textbf{Code.} A single run of \nolinkurl{ga_project/06_event_study.py}
reproduces every number, table and figure of this part (output files are
prefixed \texttt{pl\_}). The original course notebook is in
\nolinkurl{ga_project/python_labs/}.

\textbf{Event set.} The notebook's \texttt{trump\_actions} and
\texttt{country\_responses} dictionaries contain 75 events: 36 Trump actions
and 39 foreign responses (Python silently keeps only the last entry of each
duplicated date key, so two listed events are lost). Mapping the events to
trading days:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,nosep]
\item 8 events fall after 1 August 2025, when the FRED download ends, and are
dropped;
\item 16 events fall on a weekend or a market holiday and are moved to the
next trading day (e.g.\ the events of Saturday 5 and Sunday 6 April 2025 both
map to Monday 7 April);
\item 9 trading days carry two events. On 8 of them a Trump action and a
foreign response share the same day (e.g.\ 2018-07-06, 2019-08-23,
2025-03-04); on the ninth, the 4 July 2025 holiday collapses onto the
7 July action.
\end{itemize}
The remaining 67 events therefore correspond to \textbf{58 distinct event
days}, each counted once. All 58 have a full pre-event window; 51 have a full
30-day post-event window, because the last events of the sample are followed
by fewer than 30 trading days of data.

\textbf{Methodology (as required by the assignment).} Let $t_1,\dots,t_N$ be
the event days and $y_t$ a yield, or one of the three curve factors
\[
\text{level}=y_{10},\qquad
\text{slope}=y_{10}-y_{2},\qquad
\text{curvature}=2\,y_{5}-y_{2}-y_{10},
\]
where $y_m$ is the $m$-year yield. For each horizon $k=-30,\dots,+30$ trading
days, the abnormal change is
\[
\mathrm{AC}(k)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(y_{t_i+k}-y_{t_i}\bigr)
-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_k|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_k}\bigl(y_{s+k}-y_{s}\bigr),
\]
where $\mathcal{B}_k$ is the set of non-event days $s$ such that both $s$ and
$s+k$ lie more than 30 trading days away from every event. The first term is
the average change around the events; the second is the ``normal'' change
over the same horizon.

\textbf{Reading the results.} Because $\mathrm{AC}(k)$ is measured relative
to the event day, a \emph{positive} value at $k<0$ means that yields were
\emph{higher} before the event, i.e.\ they \emph{fell} into it. To avoid this
sign trap, every table reports three windows as the abnormal change in yield
\emph{over the window} (positive $=$ yields rose):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,nosep]
\item \textbf{pre-event} $[-30,0]$: $\;y_{t}-y_{t-30}$, i.e.\ $-\mathrm{AC}(-30)$;
\item \textbf{announcement} $[-1,+1]$: $\;y_{t+1}-y_{t-1}$, i.e.\ $\mathrm{AC}(+1)-\mathrm{AC}(-1)$;
\item \textbf{post-event} $[0,+30]$: $\;y_{t+30}-y_{t}$, i.e.\ $\mathrm{AC}(+30)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Assumptions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,nosep]
\item[(A1)] Business days are the trading days present in the FRED data;
weekend and holiday events move to the next trading day.
\item[(A2)] The ``reaction of targeted countries'' (Q1.3) is measured on the
\emph{US} curve on the dates of the foreign responses, since only US yields
are provided.
\item[(A3)] Q1 uses the notebook's event list, sample and baseline unchanged;
all corrections are made in Q2.1.
\item[(A4)] To judge magnitudes we also report the \emph{raw} average change
around the events with its cross-sectional standard error (s.e.). These
s.e.\ treat events as independent; since windows overlap, they overstate
precision and are only a first indication.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,nosep]
\item \textbf{No announcement effect.} Over $[-1,+1]$ the curve barely moves:
$-1.6$\,bp on the 2Y and $-0.2$\,bp on the 10Y, against raw s.e.\ of about
1.2--1.5\,bp. Nothing is distinguishable from zero.
\item \textbf{Large abnormal declines on both sides of the event.} Taken at
face value, Panel~A says that yields fall by 10--12\,bp (2Y to 10Y) in the
30 days \emph{before} the events and by a further 5--14\,bp \emph{after}
them, most at the front end. The textbook reading is a negative growth shock:
flight to quality into Treasuries and higher odds of Fed cuts, which lowers
the 2Y most.
\item \textbf{But most of it is the baseline.} Panel~B shows that the raw
change after the events is small and statistically zero: $-3.5$\,bp on the
10Y (s.e.\ 5.2) and $+1.0$\,bp on the 30Y. The ``abnormal'' declines come
mainly from subtracting a non-event baseline of $+5.5$ to $+8.5$\,bp per 30 days.
That baseline is computed on days far from any event, i.e.\ mostly 2015--16
and 2020--24, a period dominated by the 2022--23 hiking cycle, when yields
were rising.
\item \textbf{The shape confirms it.} A reaction to news would be a jump
around day 0; Figure~\ref{fig:q11} instead shows a smooth 60-day ramp that
starts a month before the announcements. This is the signature of a trend
difference between event periods (2018--19, 2025) and the baseline period,
not of a tariff effect.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Answer.} With the required methodology, Treasury yields appear to fall
around Trump's tariff actions (about $-10$ to $-14$\,bp at the 2Y--5Y over
30 days). However, there is no announcement effect, and the post-event decline
is driven by the choice of baseline rather than by the events. Q2.1 tests this
directly.

interpretation.

\textbf{Discussion.} The two mandates look very different, but for a different
reason than Panel~A alone suggests.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,nosep]
\item \textbf{First term (2018--19): no measurable reaction.} After Trump
actions the abnormal changes suggest a parallel rally of 5--7\,bp. In raw
terms, however, the 2Y, 10Y and 30Y are all within 1\,bp of zero (s.e.\
about 6--7\,bp): the apparent rally is entirely the baseline. The only clear
first-term feature is a \emph{flattening into the events} (abnormal slope
$-6.3$\,bp after actions, $-8.3$\,bp after responses). This matches the
general flattening of the 2018 hiking cycle more than tariff news. Tariffs
were narrower (mainly China, plus steel and aluminium), inflation was low, and
the Fed's reaction function dominated the curve.
\item \textbf{Second term (2025): a steepening.} Here the raw numbers move:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item After Trump actions, the 2Y falls by 18\,bp while the 30Y does not
move, giving a \emph{bull steepening} (slope $+9.2$\,bp, s.e.\ 3.0): the
front end prices Fed cuts against weaker growth.
\item After foreign responses, the 30Y \emph{rises} by 14\,bp (slope

inflationary, fiscal concerns were acute (Moody's downgrade in May, the One
Big Beautiful Bill Act in July), and markets questioned foreign demand for
Treasuries during the April ``sell America'' episode. The shock was absorbed
by the term premium rather than by lower long yields.

July 2025, so their 30-day windows overlap and represent only a handful of
independent episodes. The s.e.\ above are therefore too small, and the
differences between mandates are indicative only. Q2.1 tests which of them
survive proper inference.
\end{itemize}

\question{Q2.1 --- Limitations, improvements, and how they change Q1}

\subsection*{(a) Limitations of the design}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Biased baseline (regime effect).} The ``normal'' change is
estimated on non-event days, which fall mostly in 2015--16 and 2021--24, the
Fed hiking cycle. The 10Y non-event baseline rises by $+6.5$bp per 30 days,
while the average 30-day change was $-3.6$bp in 2017--20 and $-5.8$bp in 2025
(Table~\ref{tab:drift}). Subtracting a rising baseline from falling-rate
periods mechanically creates a negative ``abnormal'' change: a large part of
the Q1.1 post-event drift is a regime effect, not a tariff effect.
\item \textbf{Overlapping windows.} Events come in bursts (e.g.\ 14 events
between 2025-02-01 and 2025-04-10), so 30-day windows overlap and contain each
other's events. Observations are not independent, and the long windows mix
many news items.
\item \textbf{No statistical inference.} Averages are reported without
standard errors; given a daily standard deviation of about 5bp, a few bp over 30 days is
well within noise.
\item \textbf{Event-list quality.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Two entries are silently lost to duplicated dictionary keys (2025-08-06
and 2025-03-04).
\item Some entries are not shocks: ``no retaliation'' announcements, a status
quo, a US court ruling listed as a foreign response.
\item Escalations and de-escalations (Phase One deal, delays, exemptions) are
averaged together, so they offset each other.
\item Major milestones are missing: the 90-day pause (2025-04-09), the US--China
Geneva agreement (2025-05-12) and the US--EU deal (2025-07-27).
\item Some descriptions are inaccurate (145\% was the US rate on China).
\end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sample truncation.} The download stops on 2025-08-01: six
August-2025 events are dropped and late-July windows are cut short.
\item \textbf{Timing.} Several announcements came after the market close
(``Liberation Day'' on 2025-04-02 was announced after 4pm ET) or on weekends,
so day 0 is not the reaction day (Figure~\ref{fig:lib}).
\item \textbf{Confounding news.} Some event days coincide with FOMC decisions;
Trump actions and foreign responses share the same trading day seven times (eight in the extended sample).
\item \textbf{Measurement.} Level $=$ 10Y duplicates Q1.1. Constant-maturity
par yields are used rather than zero rates, and daily closes cannot isolate
an announcement from other news on the same day.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{(b) Improvements implemented}
All are in \texttt{06\_event\_study.py}, function \texttt{part\_q2}.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Cleaned and signed event list.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Duplicated keys merged; six non-events removed (list in the code).
\item Each event signed as escalation ($+1$) or de-escalation ($-1$).
\item Three missing de-escalation milestones added.
\item Result: 72 events, of which 63 escalations and 9 de-escalations.
\end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Extended sample} to 2025-10-31, so that every event has a full
window.
\item \textbf{Short windows} $[-1,+1]$ and $[0,+5]$ to capture the
announcement effect. $[-1,+1]$ also covers after-close announcements, since
the reaction then occurs on day $+1$.
\item \textbf{Local baseline.} Expected change $=$ average daily change in
the \emph{same mandate} on clean days (no event within $\pm5$ days), times
the window length. This is a constant-mean model by regime, instead of a
2015--2025 average.
\item \textbf{Placebo inference.} For each group we draw 5{,}000 sets of
pseudo-event dates (same number per mandate, from clean days of that
mandate). The two-sided $p$-value is the share of placebo averages at least
as extreme as the actual one.
\item \textbf{De-clustering.} The 30-day window uses only the first event of
each cluster (no other event in the previous 5 trading days).
\item \textbf{Robustness.} We exclude events with a same-day foreign response,
events within $\pm1$ day of an FOMC decision, and replace the 10Y by a PCA
level (first principal component of daily changes).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Overall.} The smooth 60-day patterns of Q1 are mostly macro regime
plus clustering, not tariff news. Once these are removed, three findings
remain:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item First-term tariff news had no measurable effect on Treasury yields.
\item In 2025, escalation days, and above all foreign retaliation, pushed
\emph{long} yields up and steepened the curve, consistent with a rising term
premium (inflation, fiscal and foreign-demand concerns) rather than a flight
to safety.
\item De-escalations caused a risk-on rise in front-end yields.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Remaining limitations.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Second-term groups are small (3--15 events) and concentrated in April
2025, when other forces also hit the long end (deleveraging of Treasury
basis trades, swap-spread moves), so the 2025 result is suggestive rather
than conclusive.
\item A cleaner design would use intraday Treasury futures in 30-minute
windows around each announcement and zero-coupon rather than par yields.
\item With the relevant curves, foreign yields could measure the reaction in
the targeted countries directly.
\end{itemize}
