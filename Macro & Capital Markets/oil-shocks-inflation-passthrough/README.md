# Oil shocks, inflation pass-through and the pricing of inflation risk

**Macro & Capital Markets — group project (November 2026)**

When oil prices move, how much and how fast does the move show up in realized inflation, and do markets' inflation expectations react in a way that is consistent with it? We study the United States and the euro area over 1990–2026, including the record oil shock of March 2026.

📄 **Paper:** [`paper/Oil_shocks_inflation_passthrough_paper.pdf`](paper/Oil_shocks_inflation_passthrough_paper.pdf) · 📊 **Slides:** [`presentation/Oil_shocks_inflation_presentation.pptx`](presentation/Oil_shocks_inflation_presentation.pptx)

## Main results (per +10% move in Brent)

| | Result |
|---|---|
| **Headline inflation** | +0.55 pp (US) and +0.42 pp (euro area) after 6 months |
| **Core inflation** | +0.12 pp (US) and +0.05 pp (euro area) after 6 months; much larger since 2020 |
| **5-year expectations** | +6.8 bp (US breakeven), +5.4 bp (euro area swap) |
| **5y5y forward** | ≈ 0 on average; ≈ +4 bp in 2021–23 |
| **By type of shock** | Oil-specific (supply) shocks leave the 5y5y unchanged; demand-driven oil rises move it by +6 to +13 bp |
| **2026 out-of-sample test** | A model estimated on 1990–2019 predicts US headline inflation of 3.76% in Sep 2026 (actual 3.7%); oil explains about +1.1 pp |

| Hypothesis | Verdict |
|---|---|
| H1: strong, fast pass-through to headline, weak and lagged to core | Supported |
| H2: stronger pass-through in the euro area | Rejected over the full sample (reversed since 2020) |
| H3: 5-year expectations react, the 5y5y does not | Supported, except 2021–23 and demand-driven shocks |
| H4: pass-through has changed over time | Supported (core pass-through rose after 2020) |

## How to reproduce

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
# put the Bloomberg workbook in data/ (see data/README.md), then:
./run_all.sh
```

`run_all.sh` runs the analysis (about 30 seconds), regenerates the LaTeX tables and figures, compiles the paper (if `latexmk` is installed) and refreshes the slide images. To rebuild the slides:

```bash
cd presentation && npm install && npm run build
```

The `latex/` folder can also be uploaded as-is to Overleaf.

## Methods

| Analysis | Method | Code (section of `oil_passthrough_analysis.py`) |
|---|---|---|
| Pass-through to inflation | Local projections (Jordà, 2005), horizons 0–24 months, Newey–West s.e. | A |
| Market reaction | Weekly regressions of Δ breakeven / swap on Brent returns, S&P 500 control | B |
| Market vs realized pass-through | Price-level local projections vs 5-year market moves | C |
| 2022 vs 2026 | Event comparison, 120 trading days | D |
| Out-of-sample test | Distributed-lag model estimated to 2019 / Feb 2026, simulated with the actual 2026 Brent path, bootstrap bands | E |
| Demand vs supply shocks | Sign restrictions on weekly (Brent, S&P 500) + narrative restrictions (Antolín-Díaz & Rubio-Ramírez, 2018) | F |
| State dependence and asymmetry | Interacted local projections (inflation > 3%; oil up vs down) | G |
| Robustness | WTI, month-end prices, broad commodities, lag length, crisis exclusion, data frequency | H |

**Data choices.** The oil shock is the monthly log change in the *average* Brent price (CPI measures average prices over the month); results with month-end prices are reported as a robustness check. Prices are nominal USD (deflating by CPI changes estimates by less than 0.03 pp). The October 2025 US CPI, never published because of the federal government shutdown, is filled by linear interpolation.

## Limitations

Oil is not fully exogenous; breakevens and inflation swaps contain risk and liquidity premia; the post-2020 sample is short; the euro area analysis omits natural gas prices and EUR/USD.
